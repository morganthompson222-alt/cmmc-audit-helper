Key Documents — What They Are

DocumentPlain EnglishWhy you care
FAR
Federal Acquisition Regulation		 The master rulebook for all US government purchasing. Covers everything from how to bid on contracts to what paperwork you need. Think of it as the "general terms and conditions" for selling to the government. FAR Part 52.204-21 contains the 15 Level 1 cybersecurity controls. It's the "basic" security requirement for any federal contractor.
DFARS
Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement		 Additional rules on top of the FAR that only apply to DoD contracts. More strict than the FAR because defense work is more sensitive. "Supplement" means it adds to the FAR — it doesn't replace it. DFARS 252.204-7012 is the clause that requires NIST 800-171 compliance. If a contractor has this clause, they handle CUI and must do a self-assessment. DFARS IS the enforcement mechanism — without it, the rules are just suggestions.
NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2
National Institute of Standards and Technology Special Publication 800-171, Revision 2		 A 100+ page document that lists 110 specific cybersecurity controls. "SP" = Special Publication. "800" series = computer security. "171" = this specific one about protecting CUI in non-federal systems. "Rev 2" = second version (published 2020). This is THE document. Every control in your tool comes from here. Contractors must implement all 110 controls (or justify why they don't apply).
NIST SP 800-171A Companion document to 800-171. "A" stands for Assessment. It tells assessors HOW to verify whether a contractor actually implemented the 110 controls. This is what assessors use to evaluate. You don't need to memorize it, but know it exists.
FAR 52.204-21 A specific section within the FAR. Contains 15 basic safeguarding requirements that apply to ALL federal contractors (not just DoD). These are the Level 1 controls. Even janitors with federal contracts must comply with these.
DFARS 252.204-7012 A specific clause in the DFARS. Requires safeguarding CUI and reporting cyber incidents within 72 hours. Appears in most DoD contracts that involve technical data. If a contract has this clause → the contractor handles CUI → they need Level 2. This is your quick test.
DFARS 252.204-7019 Requires contractors to submit their NIST 800-171 self-assessment score to SPRS before they can be awarded a contract. The enforcement mechanism: no SPRS score → no contract award.
DFARS 252.204-7020 Gives DoD the right to demand to see a contractor's full assessment and evidence. Contractor has 14 days to respond. This is the "show your work" clause. Self-assessment isn't self-certification without evidence.
DFARS 252.204-7021 Requires CMMC certification at a specified level. This is the clause that would have forced C3PAO assessments. Currently suspended. This was the Phase II enforcement mechanism. Suspended July 2026. May come back in revised form.

What is SPRS?

Supplier Performance Risk System. A DoD database where contractors submit their cybersecurity self-assessment scores. It lives inside a bigger system called PIEE (Procurement Integrated Enterprise Environment).

Think of it like a credit score for defense contractors, but for cybersecurity. Before awarding a contract, the DoD checks SPRS to see if the contractor has submitted their self-assessment. No submission = no contract.

Contractors access it at piee.eb.mil (requires registration).

What is an SSP?

System Security Plan. A document (typically 50-200 pages) that describes every aspect of a contractor's cybersecurity implementation. It's the primary deliverable of a self-assessment.

It answers: "Here are my systems, here's what CUI I handle, here's how I protect it, control by control, with evidence." It must be uploaded alongside the SPRS score and kept current.

What is a POA&M?

Plan of Action & Milestones. A document that lists every cybersecurity control the contractor hasn't implemented yet, along with:

  • What needs to be done to fix it
  • Who is responsible for fixing it
  • The deadline for fixing it
  • Current status (open/closed)

Every "Not Implemented" control MUST have a POA&M entry. Submitting a SPRS score without a POA&M for gaps is incomplete.

What is a C3PAO?

CMMC Third Party Assessment Organization. A company authorized by the CMMC Accreditation Body to conduct official CMMC Level 2 certification assessments. Think of them like an "authorized inspector." They come to your facility, review your evidence, test your controls, and issue a certification — or don't.

Currently suspended (July 2026). Contractors no longer need C3PAO certification for now. Self-assessment is sufficient pending the DoD review.

What is the False Claims Act?

A US federal law (31 U.S.C. §§ 3729-3733) that makes it illegal to submit false or fraudulent claims to the government. Passed during the Civil War to stop contractors from selling the Union Army defective rifles and sick mules.

In CMMC context: If a contractor tells the DoD "I've implemented all 110 NIST controls" when they haven't — that's a false claim. If they submit a SPRS score based on false information — that's a false claim.

Penalties: 3x the government's financial damages + £12,000–£28,000 per individual false claim (adjusted annually for inflation). Plus: debarment from all government contracting.

"Knowingly" means: You actually knew, OR you should have known, OR you deliberately avoided finding out. You don't need to have bad intentions — being reckless or willfully ignorant counts.

What is a Cybersecurity Control?

A "control" is just a specific security measure. Examples:

  • AC.L2-3.1.1: "Limit system access to authorized users." → You need user accounts and permissions, not shared logins.
  • IA.L2-3.5.3: "Use multifactor authentication." → You need 2-step login, not just a password.
  • SI.L2-3.14.1: "Identify, report, and correct system flaws." → You need to patch your software.

Each control has a unique ID (like AC.L2-3.1.1) that identifies which family it belongs to (AC = Access Control), what level (L2 = Level 2), and a number. Don't memorize the IDs — just know what the families are.

Common Contractor Misunderstandings

  1. "CMMC is cancelled." → No, C3PAO certification is suspended. Self-assessment is still mandatory.
  2. "I don't have CUI." → If your contract has DFARS 7012, you handle CUI. The government may not have labeled it, but the contract clause is the tell.
  3. "We use Microsoft 365/Google Workspace so we're secure." → Using a secure platform doesn't mean your configuration of it meets NIST 800-171. Default settings almost never comply.
  4. "Our IT guy said we're fine." → IT manages technology. CMMC requires documentation, evidence, and process — not just tech. Different skillset.
  5. "We'll deal with it when we get audited." → You can't get a new DoD contract without a SPRS score. The "audit" is the contract award process — you fail before you start.
  6. "One-time thing, done and dusted." → SPRS scores must be renewed every 3 years. Cyber incidents must be reported within 72 hours, every time. Continuous obligation.

2. SPRS Submission Guide

SPRS = Supplier Performance Risk System. It's where the DoD stores contractor assessment data. Every contractor subject to DFARS 7019 must submit their self-assessment score here.

Step-by-Step Submission

1
Get a PIEE account. SPRS lives inside PIEE (Procurement Integrated Enterprise Environment). Contractor registers at piee.eb.mil. Takes 1-3 business days for approval.
2
Log into SPRS. Inside PIEE, navigate to SPRS. Supplier role required. If they have contracts already, they likely have an account.
3
Complete the self-assessment. Work through all 110 controls. Mark each as Implemented / Not Implemented / Not Applicable. Document justification for each N/A.
4
Calculate the score. Start at 110. Subtract the weight of each Not Implemented control (1, 3, or 5 points). The remaining number is the SPRS score.
5
Enter score in SPRS. Fill in: assessment date, scope (enterprise-wide, system-specific, etc.), assessor name, score, and the total number of controls evaluated.
6
Upload SSP. The System Security Plan must be uploaded alongside the score. This is the full documentation showing how each control was assessed.
7
Retain evidence. DoD can request supporting evidence under DFARS 7020. Contractor has 14 days to produce it. File it, date it, keep it.

SPRS Score Formula

Control WeightExamples# of these in 800-171
1 pointBasic: training records, policies documented, awareness~30
3 pointsTechnical: session lock, audit logging, access reviews, configuration management~50
5 pointsCritical: MFA, encryption at rest/transit, incident response tested, boundary protection~30

Score = 110 - (sum of unmet control weights)

Example: Missing 2 five-point controls, 3 three-point controls, 4 one-point controls = 110 - (10 + 9 + 4) = 87

Common SPRS mistakes: Forgetting to mark controls as Not Implemented for things they genuinely don't do. Marking everything N/A for things they "think" don't apply. Submitting without an SSP. Not retaining evidence. Forgetting the POA&M for all Not Implemented controls.

3. DFARS Clauses — Full Breakdown

252.204-7012 — Safeguarding CUI & Incident Reporting

The big one. Appears in almost every contract with CUI. Three obligations:

  1. Safeguard CUI: Implement NIST SP 800-171 controls to protect CUI on contractor systems. Must flow this requirement to subcontractors.
  2. Report cyber incidents: Any cyber incident affecting CUI or the contractor's ability to perform must be reported to the DoD within 72 hours. Submit via DIBNet at dibnet.dod.mil.
  3. Preserve evidence: Maintain images of affected systems and logs for 90 days after the incident. Allow DoD access for forensic analysis.
Failure to report within 72 hours is a contract breach. The DoD can withhold payments, terminate the contract, or pursue False Claims Act action.

252.204-7019 — SPRS Submission Requirement

Contractor must have a current NIST 800-171 self-assessment score in SPRS before contract award. "Current" means completed within the last 3 years (basic assessment) or the assessment validity period.

Practical effect: If you haven't submitted a SPRS score, you're not eligible for new DoD contracts with this clause. This is the enforcement mechanism.

252.204-7020 — Evidence on Demand

DoD can request your full self-assessment documentation at any time. You have 14 calendar days to provide:

  • The SSP
  • Evidence supporting every "Implemented" claim
  • POA&M for all "Not Implemented" controls
  • Assessment methodology and scope

If you don't have it, the score can be invalidated and the contract at risk.

252.204-7021 — CMMC Certification

This was the clause that would require C3PAO certification at the contract level. Currently suspended. The implementation timeline that mapped contract requirements to CMMC levels is on hold pending the 60-day review.

Clause Detection Flowchart

How to check: Tell the contractor to search their contract PDF for "252.204". If they see 7012 → Level 2. If they see 7019 → they need SPRS. If they see none of them → they may only need Level 1 (but verify — many contracts incorporate these by reference).

4. Evidence — The Three-Layer Model

Government assessors expect evidence at three levels for every control. A policy alone is insufficient.

Layer 1 — Policy

What it is: A written document that states the rule. Must be approved, dated, and maintained.

Requirements:

  • Title, version number, effective date
  • Name and title of approver (typically CEO/CTO/CISO)
  • Scope (who and what it applies to)
  • The actual policy statements (the rules themselves)
  • Review cadence (at least annual)

Acceptable format: PDF of signed policy document. Word doc with digital signature. Screenshot of policy management system entry.

Pro tip: Most small contractors don't have formal policies. You can provide templates but must disclose: "Use at your own risk, adapt to your environment, false claims = False Claims Act violation."

Layer 2 — Implementation

What it is: Evidence showing the policy is actually configured/enforced in their systems.

6. SSP Anatomy — What a Real SSP Looks Like

An SSP (System Security Plan) is the core deliverable. A C3PAO assessor expects a specific structure. Yours should match it.

Required SSP Sections (NIST SP 800-18 Template)

  1. System Identification — System name, CAGE code, contractor name, point of contact
  2. System Description — What the system does, architecture overview, boundary description
  3. System Environment — Hardware inventory, software inventory, network topology
  4. System Interconnections — External connections, APIs, data flows with partners/cloud
  5. Information Types — What types of CUI/FCI are processed, stored, or transmitted
  6. Control Implementation — For each of the 110 controls: description of how it's implemented, responsible party, assessment method, implementation status
  7. Roles & Responsibilities — Who is responsible for each security function
  8. Laws, Regulations, Policies — Governing documents, applicable regulations
  9. Assessment Results — Summary of assessment findings, score, date
  10. POA&M Reference — Link to the POA&M for all Not Implemented controls
  11. Approval Signature — Signed by senior management (CEO, CISO, or equivalent)

Control Implementation Entry Format

Each control in the SSP should follow this format:

Control ID: AC.L2-3.1.1
Control Name: Limit system access to authorized users
Status: Implemented
Implementation Description: Access is controlled through Active Directory security groups.
  User accounts are created via HR-triggered provisioning. Privileged access requires
  manager approval and is reviewed quarterly. Default domain user group has no
  administrative rights. Service accounts are documented and limited to specific functions.
Responsible Party: IT Director, John Smith
Assessment Method: Examined AD group membership, reviewed access request forms for
  Q1-Q4 2026, verified quarterly review records.
Supporting Evidence: [Reference to attached evidence files or network location]
  - Policy: Access Control Policy v2.1 (signed 3/2026)
  - Implementation: AD group membership export (7/2026)
  - Operation: Q1+Q2 2026 quarterly access review spreadsheets
Pro tip: Many contractors submit SSPs that are 3 pages of generic statements. A real assessor expects 50-200 pages of detailed control-by-control documentation. Your tool generates a summary — make sure contractors understand they need to flesh it out.

7. Incident Reporting Under DFARS 7012

This is one of the most critical obligations and also the one contractors are least prepared for. The reporting timeline is strict.

The 72-Hour Clock

1
Discovery. The contractor becomes aware of a cyber incident. An "incident" is broadly defined — it includes any event that results in actual or potential adverse effects on the information system or the information it processes.
2
Initial report to DoD (within 72 hours). Submit via DIBNet at https://dibnet.dod.mil. Must include: company name, CAGE code, contract number(s), point of contact, description of incident, date discovered, known impact on CUI.
3
Preserve evidence (90 days). Take and retain forensic images of affected systems. Preserve all relevant logs, network traffic data, and system images for at least 90 days from submission of the incident report.
4
Cooperate with DoD. If DoD requests, allow access to affected systems for forensic analysis. Provide additional information as needed.
5
Follow-up reporting. Submit any newly discovered information about the incident. Report on remediation actions taken.

What Counts as an "Incident"?

  • Ransomware infection (even if you pay and recover)
  • Unauthorized access to a system containing CUI
  • Data exfiltration or suspected data exfiltration
  • Phishing attack that compromised credentials with CUI access
  • Insider threat activity involving CUI
  • Lost/stolen device containing unencrypted CUI
Critical: The duty to report exists even if you're not 100% sure CUI was compromised. "Suspected" unauthorized access triggers the reporting duty. Over-reporting is safer than under-reporting.

Consequences of Non-Reporting

  • Contractual: Breach of contract, possible termination for default
  • Financial: Withheld payments, False Claims Act liability if you certified compliance with 7012 but failed to report
  • Administrative: Negative past performance ratings, affecting future contract eligibility
  • Criminal: In extreme cases (willful concealment), potential criminal liability

8. Flow-Down & Subcontractors

Prime contractors must "flow down" CMMC/DFARS requirements to every subcontractor that handles CUI. This expands your addressable market dramatically.

The Flow-Down Chain

DoD → Prime Contractor → Sub Tier 1 → Sub Tier 2 → ...

Every link that handles CUI must comply. A prime can't claim Level 2 compliance if their sub doesn't meet it. The DoD will look at the weakest link.

Prime Contractor Obligations

  • Must include DFARS 7012 and 7019/7020/7021 in subcontracts where sub handles CUI
  • Must verify sub's SPRS score before awarding subcontract
  • Must be notified by sub of any cyber incidents (sub reports to prime, prime reports to DoD)
  • Must track sub compliance as part of their own SSP

Why This Matters for Your Business

Market size: For every prime contractor, there are 3-20 subcontractors. If you sell a prime, ask who their subs are. If you sell a sub, position it as "your prime is going to require this."

Selling angle: "Your prime contractor is contractually required to ensure you're compliant. When they audit their supply chain — and they will, because their contract depends on it — you want to have this done."

Subcontractor Pain Points

  • Often smaller than primes, fewer resources, less awareness
  • May not even know DFARS clauses apply to them
  • No SPRS account, no assessment history, starting from zero
  • Prime may drop them for non-compliance — existential business risk

11. Legal Risks — Deep Dive

False Claims Act (31 U.S.C. §§ 3729-3733)

The FCA makes it illegal to knowingly submit false claims to the government. "Knowingly" includes actual knowledge, deliberate ignorance, and reckless disregard for the truth. You don't have to intend to defraud — being careless with the truth is enough.

CMMC-Specific Exposure

A contractor submits a SPRS score claiming 110/110 based on your tool's "Assessment Progress" at 100%. But they marked 30 controls as "N/A" without justification, and 15 as "Implemented" when they only have policies (no implementation evidence). A DoD 7020 request reveals the gaps. The contractor faces:

  • Each false control claim = a separate false claim (potential per-claim penalties)
  • Treble (3x) damages if the government paid on the contract while relying on false compliance
  • Per-claim penalties: £13,946–£27,894 (2026 adjusted, inflation-indexed)
  • Debarment from all federal contracting

The Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative

Since October 2021, the DOJ has actively pursued cybersecurity-related False Claims Act cases through this initiative. Key cases:

  • Aerojet Rocketdyne (2022): Alleged failure to comply with NIST 800-171 while certifying compliance. Settled for $9 million.
  • Comprehensive Health Services (2022): Failed to store medical records securely while claiming NIST compliance. Settled for $930,000.
  • Verizon (2023): Alleged failure to fully implement cybersecurity controls on government contracts. Settled for $4.1 million.
  • Pennsylvania State University (2023): Failure to implement NIST 800-171 controls on DoD research contracts. Settled for $1.25 million.

These are not hypothetical. The DOJ is actively looking for these cases.

How to Protect Yourself

  1. Your T&Cs and disclaimer already do the heavy lifting. "Tool is for prep only, not certification."
  2. Never suggest a score. Don't say "this looks like you'd score 95." Let the tool calculate, but always add: "You validate this against your actual environment."
  3. Document your advice. If you tell a contractor "mark this as N/A only if X is true" — document that conversation.
  4. Don't touch their SPRS submission. They click submit, not you.
  5. Insurance. When revenue justifies it, get professional indemnity / errors & omissions insurance.

Other Legal Considerations

Contractor vs Consultant distinction: As a UK-based sole trader selling to US contractors: you are a software/service provider, not a DoD contractor yourself. UK jurisdiction applies per your T&Cs, which gives you some protection. But US contractors using your tool are subject to US law.

Minor status: Your T&Cs already disclose this. Under English law, contracts with minors are voidable. You've disclosed it — that's the right approach. Some large contractors' legal teams may ask about this. Have a parent/guardian countersign if needed.

Export controls: Your tool doesn't contain controlled technology. It provides organizational frameworks, not encryption or weapons tech. Shouldn't be an issue, but worth flagging.

Objection Handling

"CMMC got cancelled, right?"

Response: "No — the C3PAO certification requirement was suspended. Self-assessment is still mandatory. Your contract still has DFARS clauses 7012 and 7019. You still need a SPRS score. The DoD didn't cancel the obligation to protect CUI — they cancelled the expensive external audit. My tool helps with the part that's still required."

"My IT guy handles cybersecurity."

Response: "Your IT guy handles the technical controls — patching, firewalls, antivirus. My tool handles the paperwork: the SSP, the evidence organization by control, the POA&M tracking, and the SPRS submission prep. These are different things. Most IT people aren't documentation specialists — they'd rather be fixing servers than writing 50-page compliance documents."

"We're too small for this."

Response: "If you have a DoD contract, you're not too small. The controls scale — a small company just has fewer systems to secure. Level 1 is only 15 controls and takes a few hours. The DoD is specifically trying to keep small businesses in the supply chain. But the self-assessment isn't optional."

"£1,000 is a lot for software."

Response: "C3PAO certification was going to cost between £15,000 and £50,000. Pre-assessment consulting from a CMMC RPO firm starts at £5,000. False Claims Act penalties start at £11,000 per violation. £1,000 is the cheapest compliance investment you'll make — and it's one-time, not a subscription."

"We'll wait and see what the DoD decides."

Response: "That's exactly what 10,000 other contractors are thinking. When the DoD announces the new rules — which could be weeks or months from now — they're all going to scramble at once. If you knock this out now, you're ahead of the rush. Your SPRS score is current for 3 years no matter what the reform looks like."

"What if I buy and the rules change?"

Response: "The 110 NIST controls aren't changing — they've been the baseline since 2017 and the DoD confirmed they remain in effect. Even if the CMMC framework is revised, the underlying cybersecurity requirements don't go away. You're buying organization and documentation for existing obligations, not a bet on future rules."

"Can you guarantee I'll pass?"

Response: "I can guarantee your documentation will be organized and complete. I can't guarantee a passing score because you're the one implementing the controls and submitting the assessment. My tool makes sure you don't miss anything — what you do with it is up to you."
